Slide 1
Cross-Lingual Hate Speech Detection: English → Roman Urdu

What this project is about

This project asks one fundamental question: can a model trained to detect hate speech in English automatically work on Roman Urdu, without needing thousands of labelled Roman Urdu examples? This is called cross-lingual transfer learning.

What is Transfer Learning?

Transfer learning means taking a model that learned one task (hate speech in English) and applying its knowledge to a related task (hate speech in Roman Urdu). The idea is that language understanding is partially universal — knowing what hateful language looks like in one language gives you a head start in another.

What is Roman Urdu?

Roman Urdu is Urdu written using the Latin (English) alphabet instead of the Nastaliq script. It has no standardised spelling — the same word can be written 5–10 different ways by different users. It is the dominant informal writing style on Pakistani social media (Twitter, Facebook, WhatsApp broadcasts). Example: "yeh log bohat bure hain" (these people are very bad) — written differently by every user.

Why is this hard?

  • Roman Urdu does not appear in most multilingual model training corpora
  • Spelling is completely non-standard and varies per user
  • Hate signals in Roman Urdu are culturally specific (sectarian, ethnic, gender-based) and do not translate directly from English hate speech patterns
  • Labelled Roman Urdu hate speech data is extremely scarce
Why not just translate Roman Urdu to English and use an English model?
Translation is lossy — slang, culturally-specific insults, and informal Roman Urdu expressions either do not translate or are mistranslated. Machine translation systems (like Google Translate) are also not reliable for Roman Urdu precisely because of its non-standard spelling. Our approach avoids this by working directly on the raw Roman Urdu text.
Why not just use GPT-4 or a large language model?
Our research goal is to study cross-lingual transfer in a low-resource, few-shot setting using multilingual transformers. LLMs are also computationally expensive to run locally and raise data privacy concerns. Our approach (XLM-R fine-tuning) runs on a single consumer GPU (RTX 4070) and is deployable at scale.
Slide 2
Problem Statement

RQ1 — Model Capacity

We compare three models of increasing size: mBERT (179M parameters), XLM-R-base (278M), and XLM-R-large (560M). The research question is: does a larger, more capable multilingual model transfer better to Roman Urdu?

The answer turns out to be counterintuitive: XLM-R-large achieves the best English performance but the worst zero-shot Roman Urdu performance (Hate F1 = 0.005). This is called negative transfer — the model has learned English-specific hate patterns so strongly that it becomes overly conservative on Urdu text.

RQ2 — Zero-Shot Transfer

Zero-shot means applying the English-trained model directly to Roman Urdu without any Urdu examples. The hypothesis is that shared multilingual representations allow cross-lingual generalisation. Our result: it completely fails (Hate F1 < 0.07 across all models).

RQ3 — Few-Shot Mitigation

Instead of zero-shot, we provide K = 8, 16, 32, or 64 labelled Roman Urdu examples and continue training. We compare two approaches: standard fine-tuning (which collapses) and prompt-based fine-tuning (which succeeds). The key finding: standard fine-tuning collapses to predicting everything as hate even at K=64 in some settings, while prompt-based reaches Macro F1 = 0.745.

RQ4 — Fairness

False Positive Rate (FPR) = FP / (FP + TN). It measures how often the model wrongly flags safe content as hate. A high FPR on a specific demographic group means that group's non-hateful speech is disproportionately censored — a serious fairness concern for content moderation systems.

We found religion-mentioning text has FPR = 0.143 vs 0.027 overall — 5.3× higher. This is because in the English Davidson dataset, religious terminology co-occurs with hate speech more than in Roman Urdu.

What is the practical impact of a high false positive rate?
In a real content moderation system, a high FPR means innocent posts get flagged and removed. If this happens disproportionately for religious content, the system effectively censors religious discourse — a civil liberties concern. In Pakistan's social media context, this would disproportionately affect religious speech by minority communities.
Why study fairness in a cross-lingual transfer setting?
Biases present in the source-language (English) training data can transfer to the target language. A model that learned "religious words = hate" from English data will apply that spurious correlation to Roman Urdu text, even though the cultural context is completely different.
Slide 3
Dataset Description

English Source: Davidson et al. (2017)

Originally a 3-class dataset: hate speech, offensive language, and neither. We binarise it: hate speech = 1, everything else = 0. This gives us 21,159 training samples with only 5.2% hate — a highly imbalanced dataset.

Why binarise? The boundary between "offensive" and "hate speech" is subjective and annotators disagreed frequently. Collapsing to a binary task reduces annotation noise and makes the cross-lingual comparison cleaner.

Roman Urdu Target: community-datasets/roman_urdu_hate_speech

Collected from Pakistani social media. Contains 7,208 training samples and 800 development samples. Crucially, 53.5% are hate — nearly balanced. This is very different from English (5.2% hate), and this mismatch is the root cause of the few-shot collapse problem.

Why the imbalance mismatch matters

When we train on English data with class weight w_hate = 8.0 (to handle the severe 5.2% minority), we are calibrating the model for a very skewed distribution. When we then fine-tune on balanced Urdu data (53.5% hate), the class weights become inappropriate and the model's loss landscape changes fundamentally — the easiest minimum is "predict everything as hate."

Key metrics explained

MetricFormulaWhat it measures
Hate F12 × P × R / (P + R)Balance of precision and recall for hate class specifically
Macro F1(F1_hate + F1_nonhate) / 2Average F1 across both classes — penalises class imbalance
FPRFP / (FP + TN)Rate of false alarms — how often safe content is misclassified
Why use Macro F1 as the primary metric instead of accuracy?
In hate speech detection, a model that says "not hate" for every sample gets 94.8% accuracy on our English data but catches zero hate speech — completely useless. Macro F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall averaged across both classes, so it punishes the model for ignoring either class. A model that predicts all non-hate gets Macro F1 ≈ 0.5 (good F1 on non-hate class, zero on hate class, average = 0.5 approximately). A collapsed all-hate model gets Macro F1 ≈ 0.35 because non-hate F1 drops to zero.
How was the Roman Urdu dataset annotated?
The dataset was manually annotated by human annotators familiar with Roman Urdu and Pakistani social media culture. Annotation of Roman Urdu is especially challenging because the same phrase can be hateful or playful depending on context, and spelling variation means annotators may not recognise words they see for the first time. The dataset has known inter-annotator disagreement, which is reflected in some ambiguous samples remaining in the test set.

Methodology

Why XLM-R > mBERT for low-resource transfer?
(1) 250K vs 119K vocabulary — more subword coverage for rare languages.
(2) CommonCrawl (web text) vs Wikipedia — web text contains informal language, slang, and non-standard spelling that is more representative of social media.
(3) Temperature-based language sampling in training oversamples low-resource languages, giving them more representation in the model's weights.

English Fine-Tuning (Phase 1)

We fine-tune all three models on the English Davidson dataset using the HuggingFace Trainer. Key hyperparameters:

  • Class-weighted loss w = [1.0, 8.0]: missing a hate sample costs 8× more than missing a non-hate sample. Forces the model to learn hate speech features despite the 5.2% imbalance.
  • Learning rate 1×10⁻⁵: very small to preserve pre-trained multilingual representations. Large updates would destroy the cross-lingual alignment.
  • fp16 (half precision): halves VRAM usage (560M × 2 bytes = 1.12GB instead of 2.24GB). Requires AMP loss scaling to prevent gradient underflow.
  • Gradient checkpointing: trades 30% speed for 30% less memory by recomputing activations during backprop instead of storing them.

Zero-Shot Evaluation (Phase 2)

The English checkpoint is applied directly to the Roman Urdu dev set. No Roman Urdu data used. Result: Hate F1 < 0.07. The model learned English-specific hate patterns that do not generalise to Roman Urdu vocabulary and structure.

Standard K-Shot Fine-Tuning (Phase 3)

K stratified Roman Urdu samples (equal hate/non-hate) are used to continue training the English checkpoint. A new 2-neuron classification head is randomly initialised on top of the transformer. Problem: at K ≤ 32, the model collapses to predicting everything as hate even with balanced weights [1.0, 1.0].

Why collapse happens (the math): Urdu data: 53.5% hate, 46.5% non-hate K=8 batch: 4 hate, 4 non-hate, w = [1.0, 1.0] Predict all-hate: 4 hate right (loss=0) + 4 non-hate wrong (loss = 4 × large) Predict all-non-hate: 4 hate wrong (loss = 4 × large) + 4 non-hate right (loss=0) Both strategies have equal loss → gradient descent finds a degenerate minimum. The randomly-initialised head produces noisy gradients that reach this minimum in the first few steps, before discriminative features can be learned.

Prompt-Based Fine-Tuning (Phase 3, alternative)

Instead of a random classification head, we use the pre-trained MLM head which already knows how to predict masked words:

Template: "{tweet} This text is [MASK]." Verbalizer: HATE class → {"hate", "bad", "wrong", "vile", "terrible"} NON-HATE class → {"normal", "fine", "okay", "good"} p(HATE) = log-sum-exp of MLM log-probabilities over hate tokens Loss = CrossEntropy(p(HATE), p(NON-HATE), true_label)
Why this avoids collapse: The MLM head already assigns higher probability to "hate" for hateful content even before any Urdu training. The gradient signal is meaningful from step 1 — no random-init problem. At K=64 this gives Macro F1 = 0.745 vs 0.528 for standard fine-tuning.

Classical ML Baseline

  • TF-IDF: character n-grams (1–3) weighted by term frequency × inverse document frequency. A word that appears in many hate documents but few non-hate documents gets high weight. Handles Roman Urdu's spelling variation at the character level.
  • FuzzyWuzzy: computes fuzzy string similarity (0–100) between each tweet and a set of prototype hate phrases. Handles the fact that "kafir" and "kaafir" and "kafer" all mean the same thing.
  • Logistic Regression: fast, interpretable, excellent for high-dimensional sparse TF-IDF features.
Why is classical ML competitive with deep learning here?
Roman Urdu hate speech is highly lexical — specific words and phrases (slurs, derogatory terms) recur consistently across hate samples. TF-IDF directly captures this: rare hate-specific vocabulary gets high weight. With 7,208 training samples, the vocabulary is well-covered. Deep learning's advantage (understanding context and semantics) matters less when the classification signal is this lexically strong.
What is gradient accumulation and why was it needed?
Our GPU (RTX 4070, 8.6GB VRAM) cannot fit a large batch of XLM-R-large samples at once. Gradient accumulation runs 4 small batches of 8 samples each, accumulates the gradients without updating weights, then performs one update — equivalent to a single batch of 32 samples. This gives the model more stable gradient estimates while staying within the memory limit.
What is the log-sum-exp operation in the prompt-based approach?
The MLM head produces one log-probability for every token in the vocabulary. For the HATE class, we have 5 verbalizer words. We want to combine their probabilities into a single class probability. Since we are in log-space, summing probabilities requires log(sum(exp(log_p))) — this is numerically stable (avoids overflow) and correctly adds probabilities. It is equivalent to: the total probability of seeing any one of the hate words at the [MASK] position.

Reading the numbers

  • Zero-shot Hate F1 = 0.005: the model catches almost no hate speech. It learned English hate signals that simply do not appear in Roman Urdu text.
  • Standard FT Macro F1 = 0.349 at K=32: Macro F1 below 0.5 means the model has completely failed one class. FPR = 1.000 confirms it predicts everything as hate — Hate F1 is high only because the model always guesses hate.
  • Prompt FT Macro F1 = 0.745 at K=64: both classes are handled well. FPR = 0.355 means 35.5% of safe content is still wrongly flagged — not perfect but dramatically better than standard FT.
  • Classical ML Macro F1 = 0.841: outperforms all few-shot neural methods using simple word features and sufficient data.
  • Supervised Hate F1 = 0.895: this is the upper bound — what you get when you have all 7,208 Urdu labels. Everything below this is the price of limited labelled data.
Standard FT Hate F1 at K=8 is 0.697 — why is it high if the model is collapsing?
When the model predicts everything as hate, it catches 100% of actual hate (Recall = 1.0) but also flags everything safe as hate (FPR = 1.0). Because the Urdu dataset is 53.5% hate, predicting all-hate gives Hate F1 ≈ 0.697 (since precision = 53.5% and recall = 100%). This looks good in isolation but the model is useless — it cannot distinguish anything. Macro F1 = 0.350 reveals the truth: non-hate F1 is near zero.
Why does prompt-based FT perform worse than standard FT on Hate F1 at K=8, but better on Macro F1?
Standard FT at K=8 has Hate F1 = 0.697 because it predicts everything as hate (high recall, low precision). Prompt-based at K=8 has Hate F1 = 0.690 but much better Macro F1 = 0.474 because it correctly identifies some non-hate samples too (FPR drops from 0.998 to 0.801). Prompt-based is making real predictions, not just defaulting to hate. As K increases, the prompt-based advantage compounds.
If classical ML beats neural few-shot, what is the point of prompt-based fine-tuning?
Classical ML requires the full training set (7,208 samples) and does not generalise beyond its training vocabulary. Prompt-based fine-tuning achieves Macro F1 = 0.745 with only 64 labelled samples — a 113× reduction in annotation cost. In a real deployment scenario where you have very few labelled examples, prompt-based FT is the practical choice. Classical ML is the upper bound for the full-data setting, not a realistic comparison for the few-shot problem.
Slide 6
Key Findings

Finding 1: Zero-shot fails — negative transfer

All three models fail zero-shot (Hate F1 < 0.07). XLM-R-large fails most severely (0.005), despite being the best model on English. Strong English fine-tuning creates a tight English-specific decision boundary that generalises poorly to Urdu vocabulary. This is negative transfer: more training on the source task hurts target performance.

Finding 2: Standard few-shot collapses

With K ≤ 32, standard fine-tuning always predicts hate regardless of the input. With balanced weights, both "predict all-hate" and "predict all-non-hate" produce equal loss, so the randomly-initialised classification head's noisy gradients determine which degenerate solution is reached. With K=64 and balanced data, partial learning occurs but FPR remains 0.777 — too high for deployment.

Finding 3: Prompt-based FT is the best few-shot method

By leveraging the pre-trained MLM head (which already understands semantics), prompt-based FT avoids the random-init problem. At K=32, Macro F1 improves from 0.349 to 0.634 — a dramatic +0.285 improvement with the same 32 labelled samples. At K=64, Macro F1 = 0.745, competitive with much larger labelled datasets.

Finding 4: Classical ML outperforms few-shot neural on full data

TF-IDF + FuzzyWuzzy + LR achieves Hate F1 = 0.857, beating all few-shot transformer methods. This shows that Roman Urdu hate speech is highly lexical — specific vocabulary is a strong signal. The lesson: when you have sufficient labelled data, simple interpretable models can be surprisingly competitive, especially for lexically-distinctive classification tasks.

Finding 5: Fairness — religious text is disproportionately flagged

FPR for religion-mentioning text = 0.143 vs 0.027 overall (5.3× higher). Religious vocabulary in the English Davidson dataset co-occurs with hate speech (e.g., anti-religion hate). The model learned "religious words = hate signal." In Roman Urdu, religious speech is predominantly devotional and non-hateful, creating systematic false positives. This is a spurious correlation that transferred cross-lingually.

What would you do differently if you had more time/compute?
Several directions: (1) Use larger K values (128, 256) to see when prompt-based FT catches up with classical ML. (2) Try multilingual LLMs (mT5, BLOOM) which may have better Roman Urdu coverage. (3) Apply data augmentation — back-translation or paraphrasing to increase effective sample size. (4) Address the fairness issue directly with group-conditional regularisation. (5) Fine-tune on Roman Urdu orthographic variants (standardise spelling before tokenising).
How would this system be deployed in practice?
In a content moderation pipeline: (1) Run the classifier on incoming posts in real time. (2) Posts above a confidence threshold go to a human reviewer queue. (3) The few-shot prompt-based model would be used in early deployment when labels are scarce; replaced by the supervised model once enough labels accumulate. The fairness analysis would be repeated periodically to monitor for demographic disparities.
What is the significance of FPR = 0.003 for zero-shot?
An extremely low FPR with almost zero Hate F1 means the model almost never predicts hate — it defaults to predicting everything as non-hate. This is the opposite collapse: the model is too conservative. The English fine-tuning made it very good at recognising English hate signals, and since Roman Urdu text has essentially none of those signals, the model never triggers. Negative transfer in action.
Is this work generalisable to other low-resource languages?
Yes — the methodology is language-agnostic. The prompt-based approach and the few-shot experimental design apply to any low-resource language where a small number of hate speech labels can be obtained. The specific finding about class distribution mismatch (5.2% vs 53.5%) would differ for other language pairs, but the general lesson about calibrating class weights for the target distribution applies universally.

Master Q&A — Likely Examiner Questions

These questions have come up in similar NLP/deep learning presentations. Know these cold.

What is SentencePiece BPE and why does it matter for Roman Urdu?
SentencePiece Byte-Pair Encoding starts with individual characters and iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent pairs into new tokens. With a 250K vocabulary trained on web text, it creates subword pieces that can handle unknown or misspelled words by decomposing them into known subpieces. For Roman Urdu, this means "bure", "bura", "boora" are all broken into overlapping subwords that share representations, giving the model robustness to spelling variation.
What is the difference between pre-training and fine-tuning?
Pre-training: training from random weights on massive unlabelled text using self-supervised objectives (predicting masked words). Done once by large research labs (Meta, Google) — takes weeks on hundreds of GPUs. Fine-tuning: starting from pre-trained weights and continuing training on labelled task data. Done by us — takes minutes to hours on a single GPU. Fine-tuning requires far less data because the model already knows language; it just needs to learn the task-specific mapping.
What is Masked Language Modelling (MLM)?
MLM is XLM-R's pre-training objective. During pre-training, 15% of tokens in each sentence are masked (replaced with [MASK]). The model must predict what the original token was, based on the surrounding context. This forces the model to learn deep bidirectional contextual representations of language — it must understand the whole sentence to predict one word. This is why the pre-trained MLM head is useful for prompt-based fine-tuning: it already understands word semantics in context.
How did you choose the verbalizer words for prompt-based FT?
We followed Ullah et al. (2025), who validated these specific words for hate speech sentiment analysis. We additionally verified that each word is a single SentencePiece token in the XLM-R vocabulary (not split into subwords) — this matters because we read probabilities at the [MASK] position for a single token. Words that get split into multiple tokens would require more complex aggregation. We verified: "hate", "bad", "wrong", "vile", "terrible", "normal", "fine", "okay", "good" are all single tokens.
How did you ensure fair comparison between few-shot methods?
Both standard fine-tuning and prompt-based fine-tuning use exactly the same stratified K-shot splits, fixed with random seed 0/1/2. Results are mean ± std across 3 seeds. The English checkpoint used as the starting point is identical for both methods. This ensures any performance difference is due to the training method, not lucky/unlucky data selection.
What is temperature-based language sampling in XLM-R pre-training?
Without adjustment, training data would be sampled proportional to corpus size — English (dominant on the web) would be 80%+ of all training data, leaving tiny languages like Urdu with almost no representation. Temperature sampling with T=0.7 upsamples low-resource languages by sampling proportional to corpus_size^(1/T). This gives languages like Urdu ~3–5× more training examples than naive proportional sampling would allow, significantly improving their representation in the model.
