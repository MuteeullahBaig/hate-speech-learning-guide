Slide 2  ·  Problem Statement  ·  Presenter A
~2 minutes
Say — Why Roman Urdu
Roman Urdu is Urdu written using English letters — it is how most Pakistanis type informally on social media. It has no standard spelling, no grammar rules, and mixes English and Urdu words freely. Hate speech detection systems are almost entirely built for English. They are blind to hate speech in Roman Urdu.
Point to the three bullet points
Point at each bullet as you mention it
Say
There are three core challenges. First, hate speech tools are almost non-existent for Roman Urdu. Second, the spelling varies so much that the same word might be written ten different ways. And third, the cultural references and insults used in Roman Urdu hate speech simply do not appear in English training data.
Say — Research Questions
So we structured our work around four research questions.
Point to RQ1
RQ1: Does a larger model transfer better? We tested three models — mBERT, XLM-R-base, and XLM-R-large — of increasing size.
Point to RQ2
RQ2: Can a model trained only on English detect Roman Urdu hate speech with no Urdu examples at all? This is called zero-shot transfer.
Point to RQ3
RQ3: If we give the model just a handful of Urdu examples — say 8 or 64 — does it improve? And does prompt-based fine-tuning work better than standard fine-tuning?
Point to RQ4
And RQ4: Does the model treat all types of content fairly? Or does it flag certain topics — like religion or gender — as hate speech more often than others?
If asked "what is zero-shot?": Zero-shot means we apply the model to a new language without giving it any examples from that language. It has to use only what it learned from English.

Slide 3  ·  Dataset Description  ·  Presenter A
~1.5 minutes
Point to the English (source) card
Say — English dataset
For our source language, we used the Davidson et al. 2017 English hate speech dataset, loaded from HuggingFace. It contains about 21,000 training samples, but only 5.2% of them are labelled as hate speech. This severe class imbalance is an important problem we had to handle during training.
Point to the Roman Urdu (target) card
Say — Roman Urdu dataset
For our target language, we used the Roman Urdu hate speech dataset, also from HuggingFace. It has about 7,200 training samples — but here, 53.5% are hate speech. So the two datasets have completely opposite class distributions.
Say — the key challenge
This mismatch — 5.2% hate in English versus 53.5% hate in Roman Urdu — is the single most important factor in our results. When we calibrate our model for the very skewed English data and then apply it to the balanced Urdu data, the model breaks down. It no longer knows what the "typical" proportion of hate speech should be, and it starts predicting everything as hate. This is what we call few-shot collapse, which I will explain more on the next slide.
If asked "why not collect more Urdu data?": That is actually the whole point of transfer learning — collecting and labelling hate speech data is expensive, legally sensitive, and exposes annotators to harmful content. We want to minimise how much labelled data we need.

Slide 4  ·  Methodology  ·  Presenter B
~2.5 minutes
Say — Models
We tested three multilingual transformer models, all pre-trained on text from 100 languages. The smallest is mBERT, our baseline. Then XLM-R-base for ablation. And our primary model is XLM-R-large with 560 million parameters — the largest and most powerful. All three were pre-trained by Meta or Google on hundreds of gigabytes of web text, so they already understand language. We just need to teach them our specific task.
Point to the Transfer Protocols section
Say — Zero-shot
Our first protocol is zero-shot transfer: we train on English, then directly test on Roman Urdu with no Urdu examples at all. This completely fails — Hate F1 below 0.07 across all models.
Say — Standard few-shot
Our second protocol is standard few-shot fine-tuning: we take the English checkpoint and continue training it with K labelled Urdu examples — 8, 16, 32, or 64. The problem is that this collapses: the model predicts everything as hate. It gets a high hate recall, but it also flags every safe post as hate. The root cause is that we are adding a randomly-initialised classification head on top of the model, and with so few examples, the noisy gradients push the model to the easiest solution — just always predict hate.
Say — Prompt-based
Our third protocol — and our main contribution — is prompt-based fine-tuning. Instead of a random classification head, we reframe the task as fill-in-the-blank. We append the sentence "This text is [MASK]" to every tweet, and we ask the model to predict what word goes in the blank. If it predicts words like "hate", "bad", or "vile", we classify it as hate. If it predicts "normal", "fine", or "okay", it is safe. The key insight is that the model already knows what these words mean — it has seen them millions of times during pre-training. So the gradient signal is meaningful from the very first step, and the model does not collapse.
Point to the Classical ML section
Say — Classical ML
We also ran a classical machine learning baseline using the full Urdu training set. We extracted TF-IDF features — which weight words by how distinctive they are for hate speech — combined with FuzzyWuzzy similarity scores that handle Roman Urdu's spelling variation. These features were fed into a Logistic Regression classifier.
If asked about gradient checkpointing: It is a memory-saving technique. Instead of storing all intermediate computations during the forward pass, we recompute them during backpropagation. It uses 30% less memory at the cost of being 30% slower.

~2 minutes
Say — introduce the table
Here are our results on the Roman Urdu development set. The three main metrics are Hate F1 — how well we catch hate speech — Macro F1 — the average F1 across both classes, which penalises ignoring either class — and FPR, the false positive rate, which tells us how often safe content is wrongly flagged.
Point to zero-shot row
Zero-shot: Hate F1 below 0.07. Complete failure. The model catches almost nothing.
Point to standard FT row
Standard fine-tuning at K equals 64: Hate F1 is 0.715, which looks reasonable, but the FPR is 0.777 — meaning 77% of safe content is being falsely flagged as hate. The model is essentially predicting hate for almost everything. Macro F1 of 0.528 confirms this — it is not handling both classes well.
Point to prompt-based row — pause briefly
Say
Prompt-based fine-tuning at K equals 64: Hate F1 rises to 0.787, but more importantly, Macro F1 jumps to 0.745 and FPR drops to 0.355. The model is now making real predictions, not just defaulting to hate. This is a fundamentally different behaviour with the same 64 labelled samples.
Point to classical ML and supervised rows
When we have the full Urdu training set, the classical ML baseline reaches Hate F1 of 0.857 — outperforming all our few-shot neural methods. And the fully supervised XLM-R-large, trained on all 7,200 Urdu samples, achieves our best result: Hate F1 = 0.895, Macro F1 = 0.885. This is our upper bound — what is possible with full labelled data.
Key talking point: emphasise that prompt-based FT achieves 0.745 Macro F1 with only 64 examples. The classical ML baseline needs 7,208 examples to beat it — that is a 113x reduction in labelling cost.

Slide 6  ·  Key Findings  ·  Presenter B
~2 minutes
Say — introduce
Let me summarise our five key findings.
Point to Finding 1
First: zero-shot transfer completely fails, and interestingly, the largest model fails the most. XLM-R-large, our best model on English, scores nearly zero on Roman Urdu zero-shot. Stronger English training creates a tighter English-specific decision boundary that does not generalise. This is called negative transfer.
Point to Finding 2
Second: standard few-shot fine-tuning collapses to predicting everything as hate, even with balanced class weights. This is not a calibration issue — it is a fundamental problem with randomly-initialised classification heads and very few training samples.
Point to Finding 3
Third: prompt-based fine-tuning solves this. By using the pre-trained MLM head and treating classification as a fill-in-the-blank problem, we get meaningful gradient signals from the very first training step. At K equals 32, Macro F1 improves by 0.28 absolute points over standard fine-tuning.
Point to Finding 4
Fourth: when labelled data is available, a simple TF-IDF plus FuzzyWuzzy plus Logistic Regression model outperforms our few-shot neural methods. Roman Urdu hate speech has very distinctive vocabulary, so simple word-counting features work extremely well.
Point to Finding 5
Fifth: our model is not fair across all content types. Text mentioning religion is falsely flagged as hate speech five times more often than average. This is a bias inherited from the English training data, where religious language co-occurred with hate speech. Any real deployment of this system would need to address this disparity.
Say — closing
To summarise the project: cross-lingual transfer for hate speech detection is genuinely hard. Zero-shot fails. Standard few-shot collapses. But prompt-based fine-tuning provides a practical path forward — strong performance with as few as 64 labelled examples. Thank you. We are happy to take your questions.

Q&A — Quick Answers (keep these short, 30–40 seconds each)
10 minutes
If asked about negative transfer
Negative transfer means stronger training on the source task actually hurts target performance. XLM-R-large learned English hate patterns so precisely that it became overly conservative on Roman Urdu — it almost never triggers, giving Hate F1 of just 0.005.
If asked why not translate Roman Urdu to English first
Translation is unreliable for Roman Urdu because of non-standard spelling. Google Translate does not handle "kaafir" and "kafir" and "kafer" consistently. Our approach works directly on the raw text.
If asked why Macro F1 and not accuracy
A model that predicts everything as non-hate gets 94.8% accuracy on our English data but catches zero hate speech. Macro F1 is the average F1 across both classes — you need to do well on both to score well.
If asked what the verbalizer is
It is a mapping from class labels to actual words. For the hate class, we map to words like "hate", "bad", "vile". For non-hate, we map to "normal", "fine", "okay". We look at the model's probability for each of these words at the mask position and use that to make the classification decision.
If asked what would you do differently
We would explore larger K values, try multilingual LLMs like mT5, apply spelling normalisation before tokenising Roman Urdu, and address the religious text fairness issue directly with group-conditional training.
If asked why classical ML beats few-shot neural methods
Roman Urdu hate speech relies on very specific vocabulary — slurs, derogatory phrases that repeat across samples. TF-IDF directly captures this signal. The classical model also has access to all 7,208 training samples, while our few-shot models use at most 64. That is a 113-to-1 advantage in labelled data.
If asked about the fairness finding
In the English training data, religious language frequently appeared in hate speech context. The model learned this as a signal. In Roman Urdu, religious speech is predominantly devotional, so the model generates false alarms 5 times more often for religious content than for general content. This is called a spurious correlation.
